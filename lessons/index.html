Lesson 1 of 3

Why does Earth's tilt cause seasons?

By the end you can explain what makes it summer in one half of the world and winter in the other, and why distance from the Sun is not the reason.

Seasons orrery: drag to turn it, use the buttons on the leftFig. 07

Try this

  1. Start in June

    The model opens on 21 June. Look at the red tip of Earth's axis: the North Pole leans toward the Sun. The readout says the Sun is overhead at 23.5 N and a day at 45 N lasts over 15 hours.

  2. Go to December

    Press Go to ▾ and choose December solstice. The axis still points the same way in the room, but now the north leans away from the Sun. Northern days shrink to about 8½ hours.

  3. Watch a whole year

    Press Orbit a year. Keep your eye on the axis: it never turns. That is the whole secret of the seasons.

  4. Look inside

    Switch on See-through and Labels. A belt between two equal pulleys keeps the model's axis parallel, just as nothing turns Earth's real axis as it orbits.

The idea

Earth's axis is tilted by about 23.5 degrees, and it always points the same way in space. As Earth travels round the Sun, first one half and then the other leans toward the Sun.

The half leaning toward the Sun has summer for two reasons. The Sun climbs higher in the sky, so its light hits the ground more directly and each patch of ground gets more energy. And the days are longer, so there are more hours for the ground to warm up.

23.5°

Earth's tilt. It is why the Sun is overhead at 23.5 N in June and 23.5 S in December.

2 reasons

Leaning toward the Sun means more direct light and longer days.

Early Jan

When Earth is closest to the Sun: during northern winter. So distance is not the cause.

Lesson 2 of 3

How the seasons play out in the three heat zones

By the end you can name the Torrid, Temperate and Frigid Zones, say where their edges are, and describe what a year feels like in each.

Heat zones orrery: orange Torrid, green Temperate, pale FrigidFig. 08

Try this

  1. Find the zone edges

    The lines on the globe are the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn (23.5 N and S) and the Arctic and Antarctic Circles (66.5 N and S). They are exactly where the tilt puts them.

  2. Follow the overhead Sun

    Press Orbit a year and watch "Sun overhead" on the right. It swings between 23.5 N and 23.5 S and never leaves the orange Torrid Zone.

  3. Compare daylight

    Go to the June solstice and read the three daylight figures: midnight sun at 75 N, about 12½ hours at 10 N, polar night at 75 S. Then go to December and see them swap.

  4. Switch the zones off and on

    Press Heat zones to see the ordinary Earth, then press it again. Notice the frigid caps are the parts that spend the longest in the dark.

The three zones

Torrid Zone

23.5 N to 23.5 S. The only place the Sun can be straight overhead, which happens twice a year. Hot all year, with about 12 hours of daylight every day. The seasons are wet and dry rather than hot and cold.

Temperate Zones

23.5° to 66.5°, north and south. The Sun is never overhead, but its height changes a lot through the year. Strong seasons: long, warm summer days and short, cold winter days. North and south always have opposite seasons.

Frigid Zones

Beyond 66.5° N and S. The Sun stays low, so heating is weak all year. At least one day each summer it never sets (midnight sun) and one day each winter it never rises (polar night). At the poles each lasts about six months.

Why the edges are where they are

The zone edges come straight from the tilt. The Sun can only be overhead as far as 23.5° from the equator, because that is how far Earth leans. The polar circles sit 23.5° from the poles (90 − 23.5 = 66.5), because that is how far the light reaches past the pole when it leans toward the Sun, and how far the dark reaches when it leans away.

Lesson 3 of 3

Which heat zone is a city in?

By the end you can use a city's latitude to say which heat zone it is in, and predict its seasons and daylight.

London is pinned. Use City ▾ to pick anotherFig. 08

Try this

  1. Read London

    London is pinned at 51.5 N: Temperate. On 21 June the readout shows summer and over 16 hours of daylight.

  2. Pick a city in each zone

    Open City ▾ and try Singapore (Torrid), Sydney (Temperate, south) and Longyearbyen (Frigid). Each one's zone is shown in the list.

  3. Change the date

    With Sydney pinned, go to the December solstice. It is summer in Sydney when it is winter in London.

  4. Find the extremes

    Pin Longyearbyen on the June solstice (midnight sun), then the December solstice (polar night). Pin Singapore and notice its daylight hardly changes.
