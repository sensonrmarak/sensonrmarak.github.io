1. The idea of the house

Think of this house as one long, calm room with two quiet ends. In the middle is the sitting hall, the dining table and the kitchen, all under one vaulted ceiling, with no partitions between them. You define each part by a column, a change of ceiling and the furniture, so the whole space stays generous when you are alone and stretches when friends arrive. At the west end is your own bedroom suite. At the east end, as far away as the plan allows, is the guest suite. A guest and you never share a passage at night, and the guest has a shared bathroom and washroom right beside their door for friends who stay late.

The house faces south. In the Garo Hills that one decision does most of the climate work. The long south face takes the low winter sun into the sitting hall and both bedrooms, while a deep veranda with a carefully set roof edge blocks the high summer and monsoon sun completely. The service rooms, bathrooms, utility and store, are lined up along the north edge on one continuous plumbing line, where they shield the living rooms from the cold north side and keep every pipe on an outside wall you can reach.

The library, the study and the music studio are clubbed together as one creative suite at the west end, next to the front door. The library takes the steady north light it needs, then opens through a pair of glazed doors into the study. Beyond the study, through a double-door airlock, the music studio sits in its own short wing projecting north. It has floating floors, splayed walls and its own lining, so you can play at midnight while a guest sleeps at the far east end. The shared toilet and bathroom sit together in the north-east corner of the house, each with outside walls and windows. A covered walk links the garage to the front door, so you can come and go in a monsoon downpour without an umbrella.

Behind the house, across a lawn of about 36 ft, the firehouse closes the garden. It is a smaller cousin of the house, with the same white columns, charcoal roof and stone base, built around a real fire. Beyond it the land steps down through an orchard and a kitchen garden into a grove of native trees that is cheap to keep and right for the hills. The building takes up under 7 per cent of the hectare. The rest is garden, orchard and grove.

Humidity and fungus Plinth 2 ft 6 in with a damp-proof course of 1½ in plum concrete with waterproofing compound at plinth beam top. Lime render breathes; cement paint is not used on the outside.
Cold winter mornings Long axis east to west, living rooms on the south. At latitude 25.5° N the noon sun is 41° high in December, 64° at the equinoxes and 88° in June. The veranda roof eave is set at +12 ft 5 in and 10 ft 0 in out from the wall, so winter sun reaches the glass and floor (see section) while equinox and summer sun never touch the glass. Bedroom 1 also has two west windows for afternoon warmth; bedroom 2 has east windows for the morning.

3. What has been taken from your reference images

  • Reference 1: the dark charcoal roof, black window frames, white columns at the entrance and the hanging lantern under the porch. The full stone walls are kept only as a stone base up to sill level, as your brief asks, with cream lime render above.
  • Reference 2: the deep entrance with paired columns and planters either side of the door, and the idea of a house that sits in a large landscape rather than a lawn.
  • Reference 3: the honest single-storey plan with bedrooms at the ends and living space in the middle. This design keeps that idea but makes it larger, moves the bathroom out of the middle to the north edge, and drops the tub in favour of walk-in showers.
  • Your cottage plan reference (a small two-bed plan with a front stoop): the furnished floor plan is now drawn the same way. Rooms are warm yellow, verandas blue and walls solid black. Every room shows its furniture and fixtures, and each name is underlined with its size given as 17'3" X 17'6". Like that plan, the kitchen is open to the living space, marked only by a dashed line, and corridors are kept to a minimum. Its bathtub is not carried over, because your brief rules tubs out.

8. North elevation and the sun section

The section is the reason the veranda roof is a separate, flatter roof at 1 in 6 rather than a continuation of the 25° main roof: carried down at 25° the veranda eave would sit near 9 ft 6 in and shut out the winter sun. At +12 ft 5 in it lets the December sun in and still stops every ray from the equinox to the June solstice.

Water and the cloudburst route

Rainwater sump: underground RCC tank 20 ft 0 in x 12 ft 0 in x 9 ft 0 in water depth = 2,160 cu ft = 61,165 litres. At 400 litres a day for one resident, guests and the kitchen garden in the dry months, that is 153 days, which covers November to March, the dry season. The roofs fill it with only 5.1 in of rain; the year's rain off the roofs is about 15.6 lakh litres, so most of it goes on to recharge the ground.

In a cloudburst: roofs to gutters to downpipes to the 6 in storm line, then to a 3 ft x 3 ft silt trap and the sump. When the sump is full, the overflow and all paved-area run-off go to the first swale (2 ft 0 in wide x 1 ft 6 in deep, on contour, with a 1 ft 0 in bund on the downhill side), which spreads it along the slope. The swales step down to the retention pond (about 44 ft x 24 ft, 4 ft 0 in deep), then to two soakaway pits 6 ft 0 in diameter x 8 ft 0 in deep filled with graded stone, and finally through a stone-lined spillway to the natural stream on the south boundary. Nothing is sent to the neighbours or across the drive.
